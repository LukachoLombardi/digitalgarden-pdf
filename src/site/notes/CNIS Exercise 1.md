# A.1.2
## Network Traffic and Packet Loss
### 1)
Packet delay generally starts to grow relative to network load once a connection bottleneck is hit on any network layer. Most commonly, this happens on the Network and Physical Link layers in the form excessive packet queuing (which eventually leads to packets being dropped due to the queue filling up) or processing load on routing nodes as well as exhaustion of a connection's physical bandwidth limits.

### 2)
"Utilizing" bandwidth fully, as in using its entire capacity for transfer of "useful" non-protocol data, is impractical for a number of reasons. From the first sentence, it is already trivial to deduce that protocol overhead will always limit the utilization of bandwidth by the application layer, as every layer below it adds its own overhead, e.g. IP for Networking/Routing, Look ups in the local subnet (like ARP), DNS lookups and so on. One more reason is the simple fact that Computer Networks also experience packet drops due to purely physical reasons such as interference, leading to retransmissions taking up bandwith (at least with TCP). On an even more practical level: during some step in routing, there will almost always be a performance or bandwith bottleneck, and bottlenecks dictate the performance of the entire connection.

$L = 10MB; R = 100Mbit/s; D = 1800km; v = 2.5 \times 10^8 m/s$
$L = 80Mbit$
$d_{trans} = \frac{L}{R} = \frac{80Mbit}{100Mbit/s} = 0.8s = 800ms$
$d_{prop} = \frac{D}{v} = \frac{1,800,000m}{2.5 \times 10^8m/s} = 0.0072s = 7.2ms$
